1981 · Nintendo

Octopus

A 4-bit chip with 65 cells of memory runs a clock, an alarm and two games. The octopus that guards the treasure has no randomness of its own: its four tentacles move in a fixed turn, and the only chance in the game is the clock's seconds.

01 · The game

Take the treasure past the tentacles

A diver climbs down a rope from the boat at the top left to a sunken ship's chest at the bottom right, takes treasure and brings it back, while an octopus reaches for him with four tentacles.

Left: Game A, 30 seconds in. Right: the clock, where the game plays itself once a second. Both are drawn from the segments the chip lit on the kit's model of it, on MAME's drawing of the LCD.

Right moves the diver one place toward the chest and left one place back; there are five places in the water. At the chest each press of right takes a piece of treasure, a point, and the diver carries it home in a bag. Climbing back into the boat with it is worth 3 more. A diver touched by a tentacle's tip is caught, and the next one in the boat takes his place; when the third is caught the game is over.

There is no picture in the usual sense. The screen is 70 fixed shapes printed on the LCD, each one on or off. The diver at each of his six places is a shape of his own, and so is every segment of every tentacle. Animation is lighting one shape and putting out another.

02 · A replay

One tentacle moves at a time

Recorded on the kit's Sharp SM5A (kit/gnw/sm5a.js, held to MAME 0.289 instruction for instruction). A frame is kept each time the drawing routine ($0640) finishes a picture that differs from the last.

The game runs in steps. Each step moves exactly one tentacle by one segment, in a fixed order: the forked one on the left, then the third, then the second, then the short one on the right ($0100). So each tentacle moves once every four steps, and a diver who counts the steps knows when each tip can next arrive.

03 · One screen, rebuilt from memory

Every segment is one bit

Click a bit to light its segment, or a segment to find its bit. The picture is drawn from these cells alone.

The cells are the chip's memory as it finished a picture just after the replay above ends. The page turns them into segments with a port of $0640, the game's drawing routine; test_frame.js holds the port to the chip's own picture on every screen of several minutes of play.

The LCD has nine 4-bit latches for each of its two sets of segments. To draw, the game shifts a cell into the latches nine times, copies them, and shifts nine more for the second set ($0640). Twelve cells hold four segments each, and three hold the digits.

So the diver's place is not a number anywhere in memory. It is whichever of six bits is lit. Moving him is moving a bit: the chip cannot move one, so the game exchanges the carry flag with each bit along his path in turn, and the lit one travels one place ($043B to $043E, $0300). The tentacles grow and shrink the same way.

04 · The tentacles

The octopus reads the clock

A tentacle grows one segment a turn to its tip, then shrinks one segment a turn. Its only choices come from the seconds of the clock the unit keeps during the game.

There is no random number generator. The chip has a counter that steps sixteen times a second, which Popeye uses for its chances; the tentacles here never read it. The seconds are as good as random to a player, and they also mean a tentacle's behaviour depends on when you started, not on how you play.

05 · Treasure

The octopus waits while you are paid

A take of treasure is four steps: the arm reaches in, the diver stands, the arm lifts the treasure (the point), and he stands again.

Left: every shape at the chest: the diver, his three poses and the bag. Right: a caught diver, held in the middle of the octopus.

While a take is under way the diver cannot move, and the wait before each step is two passes shorter, so the tentacles come faster just when he is stuck ($005A, $00C0). On the step the treasure is lifted, and on the three steps the boat counts its 3 points in one by one, the octopus skips its turn ($0367, $0375).

Without treasure the diver cannot climb back into the boat: a press of left on the rope leaves him there ($0340). In the boat he goes back down by himself after 8 seconds, unless the forked tentacle is reaching up toward the rope ($03EB).

A caught diver struggles for nine steps ($00E9, $0700). At 200 and 500 points, if a diver has been lost, the spare divers blink for about five seconds and both come back; the game waits meanwhile ($0085, $06DC). At 999 the score goes on to 000.

06 · Speed

The game slows down at every hundred points

Move the score. The wait before each step comes from the hundreds digit plus the tens digit, so 190 is faster than 200.

Wait in Game AGame BA step takesTaking treasure

The wait for every score from 0 to 999 in Game A, from a port of $00C0; test_speed.js runs the game's own routine on the kit's model for all 1,000 scores in both games and compares. A step's length, measured on the model with the diver in the boat, is about 1,114 + 200 × wait cycles of 61 microseconds.

Game A starts with a wait of 12 passes, a step every 0.21 seconds, and Game B with 10. From hundreds and tens adding up to 2, 20 points, the two games run at the same speed, except at 100 to 109 points. The fastest, a wait of 4, comes when the two digits add up to 15 or more, and a hundred later the game is slow again.

What makes Game B harder for longer is the tentacles: in Game B a shrinking tentacle can stop at its base and grow back at once (section 04).

07 · Sound

Sound: one pin, toggled by the program

Press a button to hear the game's own sound routine, recorded at the buzzer's pin.

Each sound is the list of moments the chip's R1 pin changed while its routine ran on the kit's model ($02AE, $02A7), played here as a square wave. test_sound.js runs them again and compares.

The piezo buzzer is wired straight to one output pin, so a tone is the program flipping that pin in a loop. Every step of a game clicks ($02A7). A point, a caught diver, the buzz on each step of his struggle and the bonus's beep are one routine with a length code ($02AE); the routine is byte for byte the one in Popeye. Out of a game the unit is silent except for the alarm.

08 · Controls

Both buttons at once do nothing

Time, Game A, Game B and Alarm are read through one row of the chip's inputs ($0504), and a change counts when two readings in a row agree. Holding a game's button shows its best score; the game starts when it is let go. Left and right have pins of their own, read on each pass and between the passes of the wait ($0380): a press is acted on at once, and the wait then goes on where it stopped. Holding a button does nothing more, and holding both counts as neither, though a review from 1982 thought the diver danced.

Two test pads on the board are wired to the chip's other rows. With MAME's "Invincibility" pad held, the catch is never checked, and a held left or right repeats four times a second ($01C0, $0380). On the row MAME marks "alarm test?", one pad rings the alarm and another returns the unit to setting the time ($059E).

09 · The clock and the alarm

The clock plays the game by itself

Out of a game the unit is a clock, and the game runs in it at a step a second.

Thirty seconds of the clock, recorded on the kit's model: each frame is one redraw, once a second.

The clock keeps time on the chip's 1 Hz flag, set by a 15-bit counter of the 32,768 Hz crystal ($0200). Each second it runs a whole step of the game: the diver moves on, or back when he carries treasure, one tentacle moves and the catch is checked, with no points kept. The demo's diver is caught like any other.

Alarm shows the alarm's time, and each press turns it on or off; the note beside the clock is lit while it is on. Left and right then set its hours and minutes. When it rings, for a minute, the little octopus in the corner appears and the bolts flash.
